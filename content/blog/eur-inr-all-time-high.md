If you're planning a trip to India and you spend in euros, you've quietly become a lot wealthier this year. The EUR/INR exchange rate just hit an all-time high above 111 rupees per euro, up from around 96 a year ago. On a 2,000 euro travel budget, that's roughly 30,000 extra rupees in your pocket — enough for a week of nice hotels in Goa or several internal flights.

Here's what's actually driving the move, how confident you can be that it lasts, and what to do about it.

## The numbers

The euro reached 111.86 rupees in late April 2026, the highest level on record. Over the last six months alone, EUR/INR is up nearly 9%. Over the last twelve months, it's up over 16%. For context, the rate was sitting near 96 rupees per euro this time last year — meaning the same euro now buys 16% more Indian currency than it did a year ago.

This isn't just a rounding error. It's a genuinely large move that has compounded steadily for over a year, and it shows no clear sign of reversing in the near term.

## Why it's happening — two stories at once

Most coverage frames this as "the Indian rupee is weakening." That's true, but it's only half the story. The euro itself has also been strong against most major currencies for much of 2025 and into 2026. EUR/INR is moving up because both forces are pushing in the same direction.

**On the rupee side**, India imports about 85% of its oil, and oil prices have stayed elevated this year due to the US-Iran conflict and instability around the Strait of Hormuz. Every percentage point higher on oil prices forces India to spend more dollars to buy crude — which means selling rupees to get those dollars. That's mechanical downward pressure on the rupee that doesn't go away until oil prices fall.

Foreign investors have also been pulling money out of Indian stocks and bonds, with net outflows exceeding $19 billion in recent months. When investors sell Indian assets, they convert rupees back to dollars to leave, adding more selling pressure. The Reserve Bank of India has been intervening to slow the slide, but central banks generally cannot reverse fundamental capital flows — they can only smooth out the speed of the move.

**On the euro side**, the picture is the opposite. The European Central Bank has held rates steady at 2.15% since June 2025 and is signaling no plans to cut further. Meanwhile, the US Federal Reserve has cut rates three times and may cut again. When one central bank holds and another cuts, money tends to flow toward the holder — strengthening that currency. The euro has gained roughly 13% against the dollar in the last year for exactly this reason, and that strength has rippled outward to other currency pairs including EUR/INR.

So you have a weak rupee being pushed down by oil and capital outflows, and a strong euro being pulled up by interest rate differentials. The two effects multiply.

## Will it last?

This is the question travelers actually want answered, and there's no honest way to predict it. But you can frame the question better.

Most professional FX forecasts have the euro staying roughly where it is or modestly strengthening into mid-2026. UBS forecasts EUR/USD reaching 1.20 by mid-year, which would imply continued euro strength globally. Citi takes the other side and expects some euro weakness later in the year. Both could be right at different points.

For the rupee specifically, the consensus view among analysts is that depreciation is likely to continue absent a major shift in oil prices or capital flows. The Reserve Bank of India can slow the move but probably can't reverse it.

The honest answer for your trip: rates this favorable for European travelers don't last forever, but they also don't always reverse quickly. We've seen 12 months of steady weakening in the rupee, not a one-week spike. So if you're traveling within the next 3-6 months, the current rate is unusually generous — but not so anomalous that it's about to snap back tomorrow.

## What this means practically

If you're traveling to India in the next month, you should convert most of your spending money soon. The rate is at an all-time high; even if it goes higher, the upside is small relative to the downside risk of a sudden reversal. Lock in the bird in the hand.

If you're traveling 2-6 months out, splitting the conversion makes sense. Convert half now to lock in the current favorable rate for at least part of your trip. Leave half exposed to where the rate goes, in case the euro continues climbing. This is a hedge, not a prediction — you're trading the chance of a perfect outcome for protection against a bad one.

If you're traveling more than 6 months out, watch but don't act. A lot can happen in six months, and even a strong trend can reverse. Check the rate monthly, and convert in batches as your trip approaches.

A few specific things to avoid regardless of timing:

**Don't exchange euros at the airport in India.** Indian airport currency desks operate on margins of 5-10% above the actual rate. You're giving back a meaningful chunk of the favorable exchange rate you just got. Use ATMs in cities, or convert with your bank before traveling.

**Don't pay in euros at hotels or restaurants.** When a vendor offers "dynamic currency conversion," they set the rate themselves and it's almost always worse than your card's rate. Always pay in local rupees.

**Don't load up on rupees as cash.** Euro-zone banks tend to give worse rates on Indian rupees than ATMs in India do, because INR is a less commonly held currency in European bank vaults. Withdraw what you need from Indian ATMs once you arrive.

## The bigger picture

A common reaction to seeing "currency at all-time high" is to assume something is wrong or unstable. In this case, it mostly reflects identifiable forces: India's energy import dependence colliding with global instability, plus a euro that has reasserted itself against a slightly softer dollar. Neither of these is a crisis. The rate is high because the underlying fundamentals support it being high.

For European travelers heading to India, that's just genuinely good news. Take advantage of it carefully — convert in stages if you can, avoid airport exchanges, and don't overthink the timing within reason. A 16% windfall over the past year is more than most travelers ever consciously notice. You don't need to time the exact peak to come out far ahead of where you would have a year ago.
